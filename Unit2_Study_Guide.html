Unit 2 Study Guide — Growth & Conflict

The Summative Assessment · 1789–1877
NamePeriodDate
What I can do today: I can review the people, events, and vocabulary of Unit 2 and get ready to read historical sources and write a clear C-E-R paragraph on the Summative.

Essential Question: Who had power and freedom in America, and how has that changed over time?

Unit Theme: How did the promise of freedom and power expand for some, but deepen conflict for others?

1 How the test works

Part I — Source-Based Multiple Choice (20 questions). You will read or study 6 sources (letters, speeches, a protest, charts, and a diagram). After each source you answer questions about it. The questions ask you to: find the main idea, read a chart, figure out the author’s point of view, use a vocabulary word, and connect the source to a bigger theme. Tip: read the caption under each source — it tells you who made it, when, and why.

Part II — Build ONE argument paragraph. You choose one group and answer: “Did the United States keep its promise of freedom and power to your group between 1789 and 1877?” You plan it in four boxes — Claim · Evidence · Vocabulary · Reasoning — and the Reasoning box becomes the first sentence of your paragraph (your thesis).

2 Vocabulary to know

NationalismPride and loyalty to your whole nation. Grew after the War of 1812.
SectionalismLoyalty to your region (North or South) more than the whole country.
NullificationThe idea that a state can refuse a federal law. Caused a crisis over tariffs (1832–33).
TariffA tax on imported goods. The 1816 tariff protected new American factories.
AbolitionThe movement to end slavery completely and immediately.
Manifest DestinyThe belief that the U.S. was meant to expand across the continent.
RemovalForcing Native nations off their land (Indian Removal Act, Trail of Tears).
SuffrageThe right to vote.
SecessionWhen Southern states left the Union, starting the Civil War.
EmancipationFreeing enslaved people (Emancipation Proclamation, 1863).
CompromiseA deal where each side gives up something (Missouri Compromise, 1820).
ReconstructionThe period after the Civil War (1865–1877) rebuilding the South and the rights of freedpeople.

3 Part I — the 6 sources, and what to know

Source 1 · After the War of 1812

  • The war made Americans feel more like one nation (nationalism).
  • Because Britain’s goods were cut off, Americans built their own factories.
  • The Tariff of 1816 protected those factories — and later led to the Nullification Crisis.

Source 2 · The Trail of Tears

  • The Indian Removal Act (1830) forced Native nations west of the Mississippi.
  • About 60,000 people were removed; thousands died on the journey.
  • The Cherokee protested (Chief John Ross’s memorial) — it shows the federal government using its power to take Native land.

Source 3 · The Missouri Compromise (1820)

  • Its goal was to keep free and slave states equal so neither side controlled the Senate.
  • Every new western state could tip the balance — so the West kept causing conflict.
  • Jefferson called it a “fire bell in the night” — a warning that the division would only get worse.

Source 4 · William Lloyd Garrison, The Liberator (1831)

  • Garrison demanded immediate abolition, not slow/gradual change.
  • Southern slaveholders saw his message as a threat to their way of life.

Source 5 · Polk and Manifest Destiny (1845)

  • Manifest Destiny = the belief the U.S. was meant to expand west.
  • It justified the Mexican-American War (1846–48); the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo gave the U.S. the Mexican Cession.
  • The new land reopened the fight over slavery — could it spread into the new territories?

Source 6 · Black Soldiers in the Civil War

  • About 179,000 Black men served in the Union Army; thousands died.
  • They were paid less than white soldiers until Congress fixed it in 1864 — persistence despite discrimination.
  • Frederick Douglass said service would earn citizenship. The 14th (1868) and 15th (1870) Amendments gave citizenship and the vote — but after troops left the South in 1877, that promise was only partly kept.

4 Part II — writing your C-E-R paragraph

The question: Did the United States keep its promise of freedom and power to your group between 1789 and 1877? Pick one group:

White Americans

More white men got the vote (property rules dropped) — but women were still left out (Seneca Falls, 1848).

African Americans

From slavery (1790) → the 13th Amendment ended slavery (1865) → 14th & 15th gave citizenship and the vote — then rights were rolled back after 1877.

Mexican Americans

The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848) promised rights and property — but many lost land and safety anyway (Juan Cortina’s protest).

Native Americans

The Cherokee did everything “right” (constitution, won in the Supreme Court) and were still removed — showing who really held power.

Build your argument in four boxes: Claim (your answer) · Evidence (two specific proofs) · Vocabulary (one Unit 2 term) · Reasoning → your thesis (one strong sentence that states your answer and why your evidence proves it — this becomes the first sentence of your paragraph). Write 5–6 sentences total.

Sentence starters:
  • Claim: “The United States ______ (kept / broke / only partly kept) its promise of freedom and power to ______ …”
  • Evidence: “For example, in ______ (year), ______ …” · “A second example is ______ …”
  • Reasoning → thesis: “Between 1789 and 1877, ______’s freedom and power ______ (grew / shrank / grew then narrowed), which shows that America ______ its promise, because ______ .”

What graders look for (rubric): Claim · Evidence (two, accurate) · Reasoning (explains, doesn’t just repeat) · Vocabulary (used correctly) · Structure (a clear paragraph).

5 Final study checklist

  • I can define every vocabulary word above and use it in a sentence.
  • I can explain each of the 6 sources in one sentence.
  • I can read a chart and say what it shows and what caused it.
  • I can tell the author’s point of view and why they wrote a source.
  • I picked my CER group and know two pieces of evidence for it.
  • I can write a 5–6 sentence CER paragraph using the sentence starters.
